# Humour / Voice Bible - The Golf Addict's Guide to Family Reintegration

**Version:** v1 (bump on every lock)

## The voice in one paragraph

The deadpan voice of a British public body running an official Family Reintegration Programme for a man home from a long tour of duty at the golf club. It is calm, procedural and precise, full of induction packs, stages, assessments and case notes, and it treats ordinary family life as a foreign culture Dave has to be taught. The comedy comes from exact, recognisable UK behaviour described with a completely straight face. Now and then the official voice cracks and speaks to Dave directly, usually with a swear word; those cracks are rare, which is why they land. Sue's case notes are the second voice: short, dry, tired, and ruder than the Programme. Underneath it all the family want him back, and the book never says so out loud.

## Reference points

Comedians, books or writers whose register this sits near. Not to imitate - to
calibrate.

- Official UK leaflets and council letters: the form the jokes are poured into.
- The dry British sitcom register of *The Office* (UK) and *Detectorists*: small, true, awkward detail played straight.
- A tired spouse's WhatsApp to her sister: the register of Sue's case notes.
- Our own *Runner's Guide to Normal Conversation*: same deadpan programme, but this book is cheekier and allows swearing.

## Rules - do

- Be specific. Real objects, exact numbers, exact times, UK places. "Said he'd be back by one. Back at twenty to seven" beats "he was late".
- Base jokes on real observed behaviour, not on the idea of the behaviour.
- Let the reader do the last step. Do not explain the joke.
- Dry over loud. Understatement over exclamation.
- Keep the official voice straight; let it crack only for a punchline, and then name Dave.
- Keep Sue's case notes short, dated by programme day, and in her own plain words.
- Pay off the running jokes where the concept says: the quick nine, swinging whatever he holds, the putt on the 7th, the club committee.
- Write natural prose. Full sentences. Paragraphs that breathe.

## Rules - do not

- No generic motivational language.
- No corporate register ("leverage", "journey", "unlock").
- No sentimentality, no sanitising the joke at the end.
- Do not use the "It's not X, it's Y" construction.
- Do not use three-part aphorisms as a rhythm crutch.
- Do not stack short single-sentence paragraphs for fake emphasis.
- Do not open consecutive sections with the same rhetorical move.
- No em-dash-heavy AI cadence; vary sentence construction.
- No rhetorical questions used as filler.
- No real golf brands, clubs, courses, shops, TV channels or professional players by name.
- No jokes about real drink problems, divorce, affairs or Dave being a bad dad or husband.
- No American English or American settings.

## Swearing

Allowed, for adults, and rationed so it stays funny.

- Freely, where it fits: bloody, bollocks, sod, piss, shit, arse.
- "Fuck" at most about once per chapter, always as a punchline, usually when the official voice cracks or in Sue's notes.
- Nothing stronger. No slurs.
- Never used as an insult aimed at a family member. Dave can be called a daft sod; nobody is called anything nasty.

## Banned phrases

A living list. Add to it whenever something grating survives into a draft.

- "golf widow"
- "game-changer"
- "at the end of the day"
- "fore!" as a punchline
- "hole in one" as a pun for anything
- "tee-rrific" or any golf pun on a normal word
- "man cave"
- "boys will be boys"

## Person, tense, address

Second person, present tense, addressed to the returning golfer as "you", so any golfer can be the reader. When the Programme's voice cracks it calls him Dave. Case files, examples and Sue's notes are about Dave in the third person. Sue's notes are first person, past tense.

## Spelling and conventions

- British English throughout (-ise, colour, whilst sparingly).
- Numbers under ten spelled out except in scores, measurements and prices.
- UK words: mum, telly, tea (the evening meal), loo, boot, queue, pint, post office.
- Prices in pounds (£), distances in miles, golf distances in yards.
- Times in words or the 12-hour clock ("twenty to seven", "6.40pm"), never the 24-hour clock.
- The programme's stages are "Stage One" to "Stage Eight", capitalised.
