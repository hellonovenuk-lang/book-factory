## Front matter

### Title page

The Golf Addict's Guide to Family Reintegration

An official programme for bringing him home from the golf club, and keeping him there for more than a few hours at a time.

### Notice of issue

This programme has been issued because the people you live with have applied for your return.

The application followed a pattern. The pattern may involve Saturday mornings, Sunday mornings, Wednesday afternoons, the putt on the 7th, the state of the greens, the new committee, a golf bag in the downstairs loo and a quick nine that finished in the dark.

Receipt of this programme does not mean anybody is angry with you. Your first instinct to check whether it fits in your golf bag probably means somebody should be.

Keep the programme somewhere visible. Do not leave it in the boot of the car, in the side pocket of the bag with the tees and the broken pencils, or in the clubhouse, where it will be read aloud at the bar by a man called Keith.

### Conditions of the programme

The Family Reintegration Programme offers household guidance only. It is not golf coaching, and it has no view on your grip. Keep playing golf if golf makes you happy. The Programme simply asks that you also come home.

Any resemblance to a golfer you know is entirely intentional in spirit and entirely accidental in every way a solicitor would care about.

No golf club, course, brand or professional player has approved this programme. Several would prefer you stayed exactly where you are.

## Stage One: Arrival

### Chapter opener

Stage One: Arrival

Induction for household members returning from long-term golf.

Welcome home. Please leave your clubs at the front door. The front door, Dave. Not the downstairs loo, and not the boot of Sue's car, which she needs on Thursday.

In this stage you will be reintroduced to the people you live with, shown round the house, and asked to complete a short assessment of how far gone you are. Take your cap off for the photographs.

[Picture: chapter opener. Dave on the doorstep with his golf bag on his shoulder, cap on, as Sue holds the door open and the family look on from the hallway.]

### Why you are here

Your family has applied for your return. The application was not made lightly. It followed a review which found that you had spent 212 of the last 365 days at the golf club, and most of the other 153 telling people about it.

Nobody is taking your clubs away. The Programme exists to rebuild the skills that lapsed during your long tour of duty on the course: answering a question with the answer to that question, turning up to things that have no tee time, and finding the kitchen without asking. These skills are still in there somewhere. Sue has seen them. She describes 1994 as "a good year".

Your case sponsor is Sue. Sue has been married to you for thirty-one years and has heard about the putt on the 7th on roughly four hundred occasions, including once during a minute's silence. She will be keeping notes on your progress. You are not to read them, and you are certainly not to reply to them in the margin in pencil, as happened during the pilot scheme.

The Programme runs in eight stages. Most participants complete it. A small number go out for a quick nine halfway through Stage Four and are next seen in the clubhouse in late October.

## Stage Two: Communication

### Chapter opener

Stage Two: Communication

Talking about something that is not your round, and listening to the answer.

The most common complaint the Programme receives concerns conversation. Households report that a returning golfer can turn any question, from "Did you pick up the milk?" to "How's your mum?", into a hole-by-hole account of the morning. This stage will teach you to hear a question, answer it, and ask one back.

[Picture: chapter opener. Dave at the dinner table mid-story, using a knife and fork to show the line of a putt across his plate, while Sue, Becky and the grandchildren stare at him and their dinners go cold.]

### How a question works

A question is a request for information. It usually has a short answer. When Sue asks, "How was your day?", she is asking for a general summary, roughly the length of a weather forecast. She is not asking for the 1st, the 2nd, the drive on the 3rd that "should have been a birdie chance", or the 7th.

The Programme recommends the following method. Hear the question. Give an answer of no more than two sentences. Ask a question back, and then listen to the answer to that question, even if it is about the neighbours' extension.

### No. 04 · Quiz: What was the question?

For each question, circle the answer the family is looking for.

1. "How was your day?"
   a) "Good, thanks. How was yours?"
   b) "Well, I was one over through six, and then the 7th..."
2. "Did you remember the bread?"
   a) "Yes" or "No".
   b) "I nearly did, but Keith was on the practice green and you know what Keith's like about his takeaway."
3. "Mum's not been well."
   a) "Oh no. What's the matter?"
   b) "Sorry to hear that. Is she still in the same house? Because that's only ten minutes from the new course."
4. "What do you want for your birthday?"
   a) Something that is not a golf item.
   b) A new rangefinder.

If you circled any b), please read the method on the previous page aloud to the dog.

Since then it has been described at two Christmases, one wedding, a hospital visiting hour, a christening and a parents' evening that you attended in place of Sue and at which you were not asked about golf. ### Topics of conversation other than golf

Many returning golfers report that they no longer know what people talk about. Research carried out by the Programme with real households shows that families discuss the following, often for several minutes at a time and with nobody keeping score.

- Other people's holidays.
- What was on the telly last night, if it was not golf.
- Someone at work who has done something annoying.
- The price of things at the big Tesco compared with the small Tesco.
- Whether the car is making a noise.
- The grandchildren, in detail, including their reading books.

### Listening

Listening is the other half of conversation. It involves staying quiet while another person talks, and not using that time to plan your next shot, check the club app, or practise your takeaway with the TV remote.

A good listener makes noises such as "Oh really?" and "What did she say to that?" A good listener does not say, "That's like what happened to me on the 7th." It was not like what happened to you on the 7th. Nothing is.

## Stage Four: Time

### Chapter opener

Stage Four: Time

What "a quick nine" means to everybody else, and how to read a calendar that is not the fixture list.

Golf time runs at a different speed from family time. A round that feels like three hours to you has lasted most of a day for the people at home. This stage is about the gap between the two, and how to close it.

[Picture: chapter opener. Dave walking in through the front door in the dark, bag on his shoulder, cheerfully raising a hand in greeting, while behind him the kitchen clock shows twenty to nine and a plate of dinner sits under cling film.]

### The quick nine

"I'm just popping out for a quick nine" is the most common sentence in the Programme's files. Research shows that it is also the least accurate.

A quick nine is described by the golfer as an hour and a half. In practice it includes a drive to the club, a coffee, a chat in the car park, a warm-up on the range "to loosen off", a four-ball ahead that is "an absolute joke", nine holes, the decision to play "the back nine as well, seeing as we're out", a pint, a second pint because Keith is buying, and a debrief in the car park. Then the drive home.

[Typeset diagram: the quick nine cycle. Five stages round a circle with arrows between them: "Just popping out" to "Might as well play eighteen" to "Quick one in the bar" to "Car park debrief" to "Back home, surprised anyone noticed", and back to "Just popping out".]

## Stage Five: Family Occasions

### Chapter opener

Stage Five: Family Occasions

Birthdays, anniversaries, school sports day, and a wedding on medal day.

Families have occasions. They are arranged weeks, sometimes months, in advance. Nobody checks them against the fixture list. This stage prepares you to attend them, in full, in person, and in shoes without spikes.

[Picture: chapter opener. A family wedding photograph on church steps, everyone in their best clothes and smiling, and Dave at the end of the row in a suit, cap on, with a golf umbrella in his hand and one eye on the sky.]

### Attending an occasion

Attending means being there from the start to the finish. It does not mean arriving late "from the club", with grass on your trousers, in time for the cake. It does not mean leaving early "to catch the last few holes of the golf" on your phone, which you then watch in the car park.

When you attend an occasion please leave your phone in your pocket. The club results will still be there later. Keith's score will still be annoying.

### Occasion guidance

**Birthdays.** Give a present that is not golf-related, and has not come from the pro shop. A voucher for the pro shop is still the pro shop.

**Anniversaries.** Remember them. The date is in the family calendar, circled, with the word "ANNIVERSARY" and three exclamation marks. You will find it easier to remember if you think of it as a major.

**School events.** Sports day, nativity plays, parents' evenings. Stand with the other parents and grandparents. Do not assess the egg and spoon race for technique.

**Weddings.** Attend the ceremony, the meal and the speeches. Do not ask the best man his handicap. Do not refer to the church as "a par four from the car park".

**Funerals.** No golf. None. Not even if the deceased "would have loved to know" about the new clubhouse.

### Case study: Becky's wedding

Becky's wedding was on the second Saturday in June. The date was set a year in advance. Dave was told the date, and the date was put in the family calendar in red pen and underlined twice.

In February the club announced that the summer medal would also be on the second Saturday in June.

Dave's first words were, "Can't they move it?" It is not clear whether he meant the medal or the wedding. He then asked whether the wedding might start later, "just in case", and whether the reception was "near anywhere". Sue took him into the kitchen and closed the door. The family heard very little, except for one word, which was repeated several times.

Dave attended the wedding. He walked his daughter down the aisle and he gave a speech. The speech was very good. It contained one golf reference, which was a joke about how long it took him to get to the church, and everybody laughed, including Becky. The Programme records this as a success.

> **Case notes (Sue), Day 30.** Becky showed him the wedding photos. He said "Lovely." Then he said "Is that me with the umbrella?" and went quiet. I think it got to him. I'm not saying anything.

## Stage Six: Holidays

### Chapter opener

Stage Six: Holidays

Choosing a destination for reasons other than the courses.

A holiday is a period of rest taken by the whole family. The Programme has found that returning golfers treat holidays as an away fixture. This stage explains how to choose, book and survive a family holiday where golf is not the reason for going.

[Picture: chapter opener. The family on a beach with windbreaks and deckchairs, and Dave at the water's edge in shorts and his cap, practising his swing with a child's spade while a seagull watches.]

### How holidays used to be chosen

The Programme has examined the last eleven family holidays. Nine were within a short drive of at least one golf course. Dave says this was "a coincidence". The villa in the Algarve was near three courses. The cottage in Scotland was on one. The weekend in Wales was booked because Dave "fancied the coast", and the coast turned out to be a links course.

The remaining two holidays were chosen by Sue. On one of them Dave found a pitch and putt.

### No. 16 · Quiz: Why are we going there?

- [ ] The family have always wanted to go.
- [ ] There is a beach for the grandchildren.
- [ ] It is near a course that "has had some really good reviews".
- [ ] The villa is "only twenty minutes from three championship courses".
- [ ] Keith went last year and got a hole in one.

### Packing

Returning golfers pack as follows: clubs first, then golf shoes, then golf clothing, then golf balls, then whatever fits in the space left over, which is usually a pair of pants and a toothbrush.

For the family holiday the Programme recommends the reverse. Pack clothes for the whole week first. Then the suncream. Then something to read. If there is still space, it can stay empty.

### A week in Cornwall without clubs

This year the family went to Cornwall. The clubs were not packed. This was agreed in advance, in writing.

On the first day Dave walked on the beach and said the sand was "very fine", which the family understood was a comment on bunkers. On the second day he went to the shop for milk and came back two hours later having "had a look" at a course on the cliff. He did not play. He watched a four-ball putt out on the 18th and stood at the fence making small noises. On the third day he hired a putter for the crazy golf and lost to his grandson, who is six.

By the end of the week Dave had swum in the sea twice, eaten a pasty on the harbour wall, and been in all but one of the family photographs. The Programme records the week as a partial success.

## Stage Seven: Relapse Prevention

### Chapter opener

Stage Seven: Relapse Prevention

Triggers, warning signs and a plan for the first sunny Saturday in April.

By this stage most participants have made progress. They have attended a family occasion, mown a lawn and spoken for three minutes without mentioning the 7th. This progress is fragile. It can be undone by one sunny weekend, a text from Keith or the arrival of the new season fixture list. This stage is about recognising the danger in time.

### Your triggers

A trigger is anything that makes you want to go to the golf club when you are supposed to be at home. The Programme has identified the most common ones.

- A clear forecast for the weekend.
- A text from Keith that says "You out Saturday?"
- The clocks going forward in spring.
- Golf on the telly, especially the big tournaments, and anything with Americans shouting.
- A new driver that "everyone at the club" has.
- The words "Actually, I'm going to Mum's this weekend, so you're free to do what you like."

### Warning signs

Relapse rarely comes all at once. It builds up over several days. Families report the following warning signs.

- You begin checking the forecast more than twice a day.
- You mention the 7th again, "just in passing".
- You start leaving the car boot open "to air the clubs".
- You say "I'm not bothered about golf this weekend" twice, unprompted.
- You look at the lawn in a certain way.
- You describe the dog's walk as "a quick nine".

### The first sunny Saturday in April

Every returning golfer knows this day. The clocks have gone forward. The grass is growing. The forecast is clear and dry, with light winds from the south-west. Your phone buzzes at 7am with a message from Keith containing only the word "Well?"

The Programme's advice is not to pretend this day does not exist. Plan for it. Agree a time with the family in advance. Play nine holes, and nine holes only. Be home when you said you would be home. Then spend the afternoon with your family, doing whatever they want to do, without looking at the sky.

### Case study: the new driver

In March a new driver arrived at the club. It was bright, it was expensive, and everybody said it went twenty yards further. Dave said he "wasn't interested". He said this four times in one evening.

A week later a large parcel was delivered to the house while Dave was at work. Sue signed for it. It was long and thin and had a price on the invoice of £449. When Dave got home he said it was "a present for Keith". Keith's birthday is in November.

The driver is now in the garage with the others. Dave says it does go twenty yards further, "when I catch it right". He has caught it right once. The Programme records this as a relapse, and Sue records it as the kitchen extension being put back a year.

> **Case notes (Sue), Day 45.** Four hundred and forty-nine pounds. For a stick. He said it was "an investment". I said "In what?" He's gone very quiet. Good.

### Keith

The Programme wishes to say a word about Keith.

Keith is a member of your four-ball. He has a handicap of eighteen and plays off twelve. He is retired, lives on his own, and is at the club seven days a week. He has no one at home who has applied for his return. Keith is a nice man, and a high-risk contact.

The Programme recommends that you limit your contact with Keith during reintegration to one round a week and do not reply to messages from him sent after 9pm, which usually say "Early one tomorrow?"

### The final exam

As part of graduation you are asked to take part in one final exercise, carried out in front of the whole family at Sunday dinner.

Sue will ask you, "How was your round?" You will answer it in one sentence. The family will then ask you about the putt on the 7th, on purpose, as a test.

You may begin the story. The family will finish it for you. They know it word for word: six feet, slightly downhill, left to right, read it perfectly, struck it lovely, lipped out. They will say "lipped out" all together, as one. Then they will laugh, and you will laugh too, and that will be the last time the putt on the 7th is mentioned in this house.

The Programme expects it will be mentioned again on Boxing Day.

> **Case notes (Sue), Day 60.** Sunday dinner. He started on the 7th and we all joined in and finished it for him. Becky cried laughing. He did too. Then he helped with the washing up and asked me about my week, and listened. I'll give him his certificate. He's still a daft sod.
