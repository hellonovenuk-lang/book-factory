## The idea in one sentence

A straight-faced official programme for bringing Dave home from the golf club and teaching him how to be part of the family again.

## Target buyer

Who actually pays for this? Not a demographic - a person.

Dave's other half, who is doing bath time on her own again, or his mum, his sister, or his mate from the pub who hasn't seen him since he joined the club. They love him, they've lost more Saturdays to golf than they can count, and they want a gift that takes the piss out of him properly without starting a row.

## Gift recipient

Who receives it, and what is the moment it gets handed over?

Dave: a UK golf addict, usually a bloke aged 30 to 50, with a job, a mortgage and small children, who still manages to play three times a week, spends the rest of it talking about it, and is a bit of a geezer about the whole thing. His wife is running the house, the school run and two kids while he's on the 14th. It's handed over on Father's Day, his birthday or Christmas morning, ideally in front of the whole family, who will read the best bits out loud over the Christmas dinner.

## Recognition trigger

The "that's literally Dave" line. What specific, real behaviour will a reader
point at and say *that is exactly him*? List at least five.

1. "How was your day?" gets a hole-by-hole account of his round, including the putt on the 7th that lipped out, which he is still bloody furious about.
2. He checks the weather forecast only to see if Saturday's tee time is on. He has never once checked it for a family barbecue.
3. He "pops out for a quick nine" and comes back five hours and four pints later, surprised anyone noticed.
4. He practises his swing with whatever he's holding: an umbrella in the queue at Tesco, a rolled-up Sunday paper, the barbecue tongs.
5. He knows the date of the club medal and the Ryder Cup for the next three years, and has no idea when his anniversary is.
6. The family holiday to Portugal was chosen because there "happened to be" three courses within ten minutes of the villa, and the kids' club opened at nine.
7. The garage, the boot of the car and the downstairs loo all hold golf gear, and none of it may be moved.
8. He watches golf on the telly on Sunday afternoon after playing golf all Sunday morning, then falls asleep in front of it.
9. He has a strong opinion on the club committee, the state of the greens and the new members, and none on what's for tea.
10. He books a 7am tee time so he's "back before the kids are up". The kids are up at twenty to six, and so is his wife.

## Humour angle

What is the comedic frame? (Parody manual? Rehabilitation programme? Field
guide? Medical textbook?) Why is that frame funnier than just "jokes about X"?

The book is the official Family Reintegration Programme for a man coming home after a long tour of duty at the golf club. It's written in the deadpan voice of a British public body: induction packs, orientation sessions, household briefings, a risk assessment, a relapse-prevention plan and a certificate at the end. Treating Dave walking in from the 18th green like a soldier home from a posting makes every ordinary thing at home (a conversation, a kid's birthday party, the lawnmower) a foreign culture he has to be taught from scratch.

The humour is a notch above gentle: cheekier and more pointed, written for adults. Some swearing is allowed, used for punchlines rather than scattered everywhere, and the official voice cracking now and then ("Dave, for fuck's sake, it's your daughter's wedding") is part of the joke. The straight face still does most of the work, and the reader supplies the recognition.

Golf gifts sell all year on Amazon UK, peaking at Christmas and Father's Day in June. Buyers search for funny golf gifts for men, golf gifts for dad, golf gifts for him and rude golf books. It sits beside novelty golf books and joke manuals, with more real content inside than most of them. The title explains the joke at thumbnail size. Pricing in pounds is a later KDP decision.

## Constraints

Things this book must not do. Content limits, tone limits, anything that would
embarrass the buyer when it is opened at Christmas in front of their family.

UK throughout: British spelling, British words (mum, telly, pint, tea for the evening meal, the missus only in Dave's own voice), UK settings and prices in pounds. Adults only, with swearing allowed but rationed: bloody, bollocks, sod, piss and shit freely; the odd "fuck" saved for a punchline; nothing stronger, and never used as an insult aimed at the family. Pints at the 19th hole are fine; nothing about real drink problems. Affectionate underneath: Dave is a loveable pain in the arse, never a bad husband or dad, and there is no divorce or marriage-breakdown humour. No real golf brands, clubs, courses, shops, TV channels or professional players by name, and no logos in the pictures. Generated art contains no text; all words are real type.
