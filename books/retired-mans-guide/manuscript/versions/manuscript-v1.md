### Title page

The Newly Retired Man's Guide to Being Home All Day

An Official Handbook for Him, and for the Person Who Now Lives With Him

### Letter of appointment

Dear new starter,

We are pleased to confirm your appointment to the Household, with effect from the Monday after your leaving do. The position is full time, unpaid and permanent. There is no probation for the house itself, which is staying where it is. There is a probation period for you.

You will report to the Head of Household Operations, who has held the post since 1985 and has run it, by every available measure, extremely well. You were not on the interview panel for her job, and you are not on it now.

Please read this handbook in your first week. It explains how the Household works, what is expected of a new starter, and why the colander is not where it was. We know you have forty-one years of experience. We are going to ask you to keep most of it in the loft.

Yours,

Household Operations

### Terms and conditions

This handbook covers life at home only. It is not financial advice and has no view on any spreadsheet you have built about money. It does have a view on the other spreadsheet, the one about the bins.

Any resemblance to a retired man you know is entirely intentional in spirit and entirely accidental in every way a solicitor would care about.

No employer, garden centre or council recycling department has approved this handbook. Several of them have met you.

## Stage One: Welcome to the Household

### Chapter opener

Stage One: Welcome to the Household

Who runs what, where you fit in, and how keen you have been so far.

Every new starter needs an induction, however senior they were in their last job. In this stage we will explain the organisation you have joined, introduce your new colleagues and ask you to assess your first few days honestly. Your own assessment will be compared with Sue's, and hers will be the one that counts.

You can take the tape measure off for the induction, Graham. Nothing in this room needs measuring.

[Picture: chapter opener. ### Your new position

Congratulations on your new position. After forty-one years in operations you have joined the Household, a small but well-established organisation that has run without interruption since 1985. You may have assumed you were coming in to manage it. Please read this handbook before acting on that assumption.

The Household is led by Sue, Head of Household Operations. Sue has no job title, no annual review and no deputy, and in thirty-nine years she has never once been late with a birthday card. She runs the kitchen, the diary, the garden, the family WhatsApp group, the Christmas list for nine people and Biscuit, who is eleven. You will report to her. Biscuit, for now, will not report to you.

Your first week will feel strange. You will wake at 6:15 because your body still thinks there is a train to catch. You will come downstairs, stand in the kitchen in your gilet, and ask what we are doing today. This is normal, and everyone who has joined the Household from full-time work has asked it. The answer, on most weekdays, is nothing in particular, and Sue would like to keep it that way.

## Stage Two: Your First Week

### Chapter opener

Stage Two: Your First Week

The alarm, the agenda and the very long Tuesday.

In this stage we look at your first week at home, hour by hour, and at the things your job used to give you that you are now trying to find in the house. Most new starters find the first week the hardest. Sue found it quite hard too.

[Picture: chapter opener. Graham fully dressed at the bedroom window at dawn, holding a mug of tea and looking out at the empty street, his old work bag still standing by the bedroom door, while Sue is fast asleep with the duvet pulled over her head and Biscuit lies across the foot of the bed.]

### The 6:15 alarm

For forty-one years your alarm went off at 6:15, so you could catch the 7:02. You have now retired, and there is no 7:02. Your alarm still goes off at 6:15.

You have explained that it is "easier to keep the routine". This would be fine if you then lay quietly in bed until a reasonable hour. Instead you get up, shower, shave, put on the gilet and go downstairs, where you empty the dishwasher at a volume Sue describes as "a brass band". By seven, the house is awake. Biscuit is awake. On Mondays and Thursdays, the neighbours' baby is awake.

The Household would like you to know that you are allowed to switch the alarm off. Nobody will mark you late. The 7:02 has carried on without you, and it has been fine.

### A week in the life

The following record covers your first full week at home, kept by Sue with Biscuit's assistance.

**Monday, 6:15.** Alarm. Graham is downstairs by 6:40 and has emptied the dishwasher by 6:44.

**Monday, 9:30.** Graham asks Sue for "a quick catch-up". She is in the bath.

**Tuesday, 10am.** Graham reorganises the cupboard under the sink and finds three of the same spray. He tells Sue it is "a stock control issue".

**Wednesday, 11am.** Graham sends Sue a calendar invitation for the big shop. It has a location, an agenda and a reminder fifteen minutes before.

**Thursday, 2pm.** Graham asks the postman how many houses are on his round. The postman tells him. They talk for twenty-five minutes.

**Friday, 4pm.** Graham says, for the first time, "I don't know how you fill your days." Sue says she fills them very well, thank you, and that until Monday she filled them on her own.

**Saturday.** Graham says he is looking forward to the weekend. Sue asks him how he can tell.

## Stage Three: The Kitchen

### Chapter opener

Stage Three: The Kitchen

The most important room in the Household, and the one with the most rules you didn't know about.

For forty years the kitchen has run very well without you. You have used it at breakfast, at tea and to find biscuits. It now has a second person in it from seven in the morning, and that person has a tape measure on his belt. Sue has put the colander back where it was. Please leave it there.

### The cupboard reorganisation

On your third day at home, you reorganised the kitchen cupboards. You did it while Sue was at Pilates, so that it would be "a nice surprise".

The tins are now in date order, with labels facing out. The mugs are grouped by size. It has a place. The place is the second cupboard on the left, bottom shelf, behind the big saucepan. It has been there since 1985. You moved it on the grounds that it "should be nearer the sink". It now lives nearer the sink, on Tuesdays and Fridays, and somewhere else the rest of the week.

The Household asks that the colander goes back to the second cupboard on the left, and stays there. You may visit it whenever you like.

### The Dishwasher Loading Review

The Household now has two methods for loading the dishwasher. Only one is correct, and it has never been yours.

Sue's method has worked since the dishwasher arrived in 2014. Plates at the back, bowls on the top, cutlery in the basket, the big pan done by hand. Your method involves a diagram. You also reload the dishwasher after Sue has loaded it. You do this quietly, while saying "I'm not criticising". The Household has looked into it, and you are criticising.

### Helping

Since you retired, you have been helping in the kitchen. The Household is grateful for any help. It would like to define it.

Helping is doing a job that needs doing, in the way it is usually done, and then stopping. Helping is not standing behind Sue while she makes the tea and asking what she's making. It is not tasting the gravy and adding salt. It is not suggesting the potatoes would be quicker in smaller pieces, or that the oven "runs hot", or that she might like to try a system. It is not putting the kettle on and then asking where the tea is kept.

If you are not sure whether you are helping, look at Sue. If she has stopped what she is doing and is holding a wooden spoon very still, you are not.

### The shed

The shed began in March. It was going to be "a simple little shed" at the bottom of the garden, for the lawnmower and some tools. You found a flat-pack one online for £340 and decided you could build a better one yourself for less.

The shed is now in its seventh month. It has a concrete base, four walls, a window, a door that sticks and a plan pinned to one wall that has been redrawn nine times. It does not yet have a roof. It has cost £1,180 so far. The lawnmower is still in the garage.

### The loft

Sue would like the loft cleared. She would also like it noted that she has been suggesting this since 1998, and that you said you would do it "when you had more time". You now have more time.

The Household asks that the tip is a place you take things to. It is not a shop.

Ellie, the nursery run, and the children's phones.

You know the names of the other grandparents in the car park, and you know which ones arrive late.

On one occasion you made Ellie a laminated card showing the journey, with times. She drew a horse on it. You have kept it.
