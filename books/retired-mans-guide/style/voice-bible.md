## The voice in one paragraph

The deadpan voice of Household Operations, the HR department of a small, long-established organisation, issuing an official induction handbook to its newest starter: a man who has just retired after forty-one years in operations and assumed he was coming home to manage the place. The structure is pure workplace induction: a welcome, a Day 1 assessment, an org chart, stages, a Project Register, a probation review and a signed Treaty. The sentences are plain, calm and precise, so the jokes read clearly. The comedy comes from exact, recognisable UK home life (the dishwasher, the cupboards, the tip, the big shop) measured and reported with a straight face, often with an exact made-up number or time. Now and then the voice cracks and speaks to Graham by name; those cracks are rare, which is why they land. The second voice is Sue's notes: short, dated, dry and specific, the notes of a woman who has run this house beautifully for decades and is mostly amused. Biscuit the dog is a silent witness who reacts but never speaks. Underneath it all, Graham and Sue are a happy couple, and the book never says so out loud.

## Reference points

Comedians, books or writers whose register this sits near. Not to imitate - to
calibrate.

- A real new-starter induction pack or staff handbook: the form the jokes are poured into.
- The dry British sitcom register of *The Office* (UK) and *W1A*: small, true, awkward detail played straight.
- A capable wife's text to her sister about her husband's first week at home: the register of Sue's notes.
- Alan Bennett's ear for ordinary domestic speech: the warmth under the dryness.
- Our own *Golf Addict's Guide* and *Padel Addict's Guide*: the same deadpan programme shape, but this one is set entirely at home and about a life change rather than a hobby.

## Rules - do

- Be specific. Real objects, exact numbers, exact times. "The weekend of 14 March" beats "a while ago"; "the big tin of beans is better value per hundred grams" beats "he comments on prices". Name everyday UK places and things freely (the tip, the garden centre, nursery, the big shop); never name a real brand, shop, firm or app.
- Base jokes on real observed behaviour, not on the idea of the behaviour.
- Let the reader do the last step. Do not explain the joke.
- Dry over loud. Understatement over exclamation.
- Use the induction frame for structure and headings (stages, assessments, the org chart, probation, the Treaty), and keep the sentences plain.
- Report Graham's office habits at home flatly, as if the Household were a workplace he has joined as a junior.
- Keep the household in the joke too: Sue's systems, Biscuit's routine and Ellie's demands are treated with the same seriousness as Graham's.
- Keep Graham capable and energetic. The joke is where he points the energy.
- Slightly rude is fine where it is earned.
- Write natural prose. Full sentences. Paragraphs that breathe.

## Rules - do not

- No generic motivational language.
- No corporate register used straight ("leverage", "journey", "unlock", "synergy"). Office jargon may only appear as a quoted thing Graham says, and sparingly.
- No sentimentality, no sanitising the joke at the end.
- Do not use the "It's not X, it's Y" construction.
- Do not use three-part aphorisms as a rhythm crutch.
- Do not stack short single-sentence paragraphs for fake emphasis.
- Do not open consecutive sections with the same rhetorical move.
- No em-dash-heavy AI cadence; vary sentence construction.
- No rhetorical questions used as filler.
- Nothing about money worries, pensions, health, bodies, memory, hospitals, ageing or death, as fixed at intake.
- No "old man" jokes: nothing about being slow, deaf, forgetful, past it or out of touch with technology.
- Never make Sue a nag or Graham useless or unwanted. No divorce or marriage-breakdown humour.
- Swearing is rare and mild (bloody, sod), saved for a punchline, and never aimed at Sue or the family.

## Banned phrases

A living list. Add to it whenever something grating survives into a draft.

- "under my feet"
- "her indoors" or "the old ball and chain"
- "golden years"
- "Silver surfer", "OAP", "pensioner", "old codger", "senior moment"
- "twilight years" or "over the hill"
- "happy wife, happy life"
- "man cave"
- "at the end of the day"
- "let's circle back"
- "the best is yet to come"

## Person, tense, address

Second person, present tense, addressed to the newly retired man as "you", so any newly retired man can be the reader. When the handbook's voice cracks it calls him Graham. Case examples are about Graham in the third person. Sue's notes are first person, past tense, headed "Note from Household Operations" with a day, week or stage ("day one", "week three"), and set as a quote block.

## Spelling and conventions

- British English throughout (-ise, colour, whilst sparingly).
- Numbers under ten spelled out except in scores, measurements, percentages, dates and prices.
- UK words: mum, telly, tea (the evening meal), the tip, the big shop, nursery, the bins, garden centre, loft, shed, queue.
- Prices in pounds (£), distances in miles.
- Times in words or the 12-hour clock ("quarter to seven", "6:15"), never the 24-hour clock.
- The handbook's chapters are "Stage One" to "Stage Nine", capitalised.
- Household roles are capitalised as job titles: Head of Household Operations, Household Operations, the Household.
