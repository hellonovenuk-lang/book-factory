# Concept - The Newly Retired Man's Guide to Being Home All Day

> Locked at concept lock. Later stages may not renegotiate what is written here.

## Premise

Graham, 66, has just retired after forty-one years as a regional operations manager. He believes he has been given the gift of time. The household believes it has been given a colleague. He arrived expecting to manage the place. He has in fact joined the Household as its newest starter, in an organisation Sue has run calmly and well since 1985. This book is his official induction handbook, issued by Household Operations. It runs from his first day to the end of his probation and finishes with a Retirement Treaty, signed by both parties and countersigned by the dog.

## Structure of the book

How does the reader move through it? (Programme? Stages? Chapters as escalating
severity? A diagnosis-to-cure arc?)

An induction programme in stages, each a chapter with an opener picture:

1. Welcome to the Household: who runs what, the organisation chart (Sue at the top, the dog above Graham), and a Day 1 assessment: "Are you ready to be home?"
2. Your First Week: the 6:15 alarm, "what are we doing today?", the agenda nobody asked for, and the shock of the weekday.
3. The Kitchen: the Kitchen Boundary Agreement, the cupboard reorganisation inquiry, the Dishwasher Loading Review and who is allowed to touch the tea towels.
4. Projects: the Project Register, every "quick job" with its estimate and its actual time, the shed, the loft and the trip to the tip.
5. Out and About: the big shop, the garden centre, the post office queue, the neighbours and the bins.
6. Family Duties: the granddaughter (Ellie, 3), "the logistics" of the nursery run, and phone calls to the grown-up children about the loft.
7. Keeping Busy: hobbies tried and dropped, old colleagues ("busier than ever"), the weather briefing, and finding something of his own.
8. Your Probation Review: a diagnostic of his new routines, Sue's feedback and a week-by-week adjustment plan.
9. The Retirement Treaty: a signed agreement on the kitchen, the projects and Tuesday, with a cut-out Certificate of Successful Retirement.

Each chapter mixes short text pages, checklists, diagnostic tests, case notes, comparisons and activity panels (ticks, scores, a gauge, a table), with the picture budget spent on chapter openers.

## Running jokes

Bits that reappear. Note where each one pays off.

- The colander: since the cupboards were reorganised, nobody can find it. It turns up in every chapter somewhere new. Pays off in the Treaty, where Sue puts it back where it always was and Graham signs a clause agreeing never to move it.
- The Project Register: every "quick job" is logged with an estimate and an actual time, and the gap grows chapter by chapter. Pays off at Probation, where the shed is finally finished and he immediately proposes a second shed.
- "What are we doing today?": asked every morning, with the time noted. Pays off in the Treaty, when Sue asks it first and he hasn't got an answer, and enjoys it.
- The 6:15 alarm: it keeps going off for a job that no longer exists. Pays off at Probation when he sleeps until 7:40 and reports it as a personal best.
- The org chart: Biscuit the dog stays one place above Graham throughout. Pays off when Ellie promotes him to second-in-command, below her.
- "Busier than ever": what he tells every former colleague on the phone, followed each time by what he was actually busy with.

## What this book is NOT

A real guide to retirement planning, money, pensions or health; a book about a marriage in trouble; or jokes about getting old. Retirement is the setting; the house, the routines and the people he now shares them with are the subject.
