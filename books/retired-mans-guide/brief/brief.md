## The idea in one sentence

A deadpan official induction handbook for a man who has just retired and is now home all day, and for the wife who has run that home perfectly well without him for forty years.

## Target buyer

Who actually pays for this? Not a demographic - a person.

Sue: Graham's wife, who has had the house to herself on weekdays since 1985 and now finds him standing behind her in the kitchen asking what the plan is. She loves him. She would also like Tuesday back. Close behind: their grown-up children, who get the phone calls about the loft; and his old team at work, clubbing together for a leaving present that says what everyone in the office is thinking about Sue.

## Gift recipient

Who receives it, and what is the moment it gets handed over?

Graham: a UK man in his mid-sixties who has just stopped work after about forty years, with plenty of energy, plenty of opinions and, for the first time in his adult life, no meetings. It gets handed over at his leaving do, with the card everyone signed, or at his first birthday, Christmas or Father's Day as a retired man, usually read aloud by Sue to the rest of the family.

## Recognition trigger

The "that's literally Dave" line. What specific, real behaviour will a reader
point at and say *that is exactly him*? List at least five.

1. He has reorganised the kitchen cupboards "so they make sense", and now nobody else can find the colander.
2. He reloads the dishwasher after someone else has loaded it, quietly, while explaining that he isn't criticising.
3. He has announced a "project" (the shed, the loft, the garage) that was meant to take a weekend and is still open in March.
4. He follows Sue round the supermarket with suggestions about the route, the trolley and the price of butter per hundred grams.
5. He asks "what are we doing today?" at seven in the morning, as if the day needs an agenda.
6. He still wakes at 6:15 for a commute that no longer exists, and would like everyone to know he is up.
7. He keeps a spreadsheet of the bin collection days and has views on the neighbours' recycling.
8. He calls the nursery run with his granddaughter "the logistics" and arrives eleven minutes early.
9. He tells former colleagues on the phone that he is "busier than ever", and he means the bins.
10. He watches the weather forecast like a briefing, then reports it to the household at lunch.

## Humour angle

What is the comedic frame? (Parody manual? Rehabilitation programme? Field
guide? Medical textbook?) Why is that frame funnier than just "jokes about X"?

Graham has spent forty-one years in operations, so the book speaks his language back to him. He thinks he has retired. Sue has quietly enrolled him in a new organisation: the Household, a long-established and well-run operation headed for decades by Sue. This book is his official induction pack as a new starter. It comes with a Day 1 assessment, a probation period, the Kitchen Boundary Agreement, a Project Register for every "quick job", a review of his new routines, a week-by-week adjustment plan and, at the end, a signed Retirement Treaty. The joke is that he arrived expecting to be the manager and has joined as the junior. Treating the house as a workplace he has to fit into, rather than one he has come to improve, turns every helpful instinct he has into evidence.

The humour is medium: dry, warm and written for adults, with the straight face doing the work. The household is in on the joke too: Sue's systems, the dog's routine and the grandchild's demands are all treated with the same seriousness, so he is never the only target. A little mild swearing is allowed for a punchline, as in the Golf book.

## Commercial rationale

Why does this sell on Amazon? What does a buyer search for? What does it sit
next to on a shelf? What is the price point?

Retirement comes with a built-in gift moment, the leaving present, and it happens every month of the year, not just at Christmas. Buyers search for retirement gifts for men, funny retirement gifts, retirement books for men and leaving gifts for colleagues. The 30 September Amazon lists show retirement gift books from small publishers selling at $13-15, with none in our deadpan official-manual style, so a £9.99 / $9.99 book is the funny, cheaper choice. It sits beside retirement activity and joke books, and beside our own Golf and Padel Guides as a book for the same buyer, the wife. The title tells the joke at thumbnail size.

## Constraints

Things this book must not do. Content limits, tone limits, anything that would
embarrass the buyer when it is opened at Christmas in front of their family.

UK throughout: British spelling, British words (mum, tea for the evening meal, the tip, the big shop) and prices in pounds. For adults, but mild enough to open at a leaving do in front of the managing director: swearing is rare and mild (bloody, sod), used only for a punchline and never aimed at Sue or the family. Nothing about money worries, pensions running short, health decline, ageing bodies, memory, hospitals or death, as fixed at intake. No "old man" jokes: he is capable, fit and busy, just aimed at the wrong things. Affectionate underneath: Graham and Sue are a happy couple and the book is on both their sides. No divorce or marriage-breakdown humour. His old employer is never a real firm, and no real brands, shops or apps by name. Generated art contains no text; all words are real type. ## Visual concept (first thoughts)

Rough direction only - the visual bible is where this gets decided properly.

Classic editorial caricature in a fine black ink line with grey washes for the black and white interior, matching the Golf and Padel books. Graham is drawn the same in every picture: 66, trim, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, a zip-up fleece gilet and a tape measure clipped to his belt. Sue is drawn calm and capable, usually holding the thing he has just moved. Chapter openers show him at home applying office habits where they don't belong: a clipboard in the kitchen, a whiteboard in the shed, a briefing for the dog. The cover is one family scene with the big title over a plain band, as decided at intake.
