## The voice in one paragraph

The deadpan voice of a consultancy running a formal engagement on one man's padel talk, commissioned by his two main stakeholders, Home and Work. The structure is pure consultancy: phases, stakeholder interviews, a current-state assessment, budgets, a risk register and a sign-off. The sentences are plain, calm and precise, so the jokes read clearly. The comedy comes from exact, recognisable UK behaviour at home and in the office, measured and reported with a completely straight face, often with a made-up but exact statistic. Now and then the voice cracks and speaks to Josh by name; those cracks are rare, which is why they land. Stakeholder feedback is the second voice: Lauren's notes are tired, dry and specific about the children; Priya's are short, clipped and faintly murderous about tennis. Underneath it all everyone likes Josh, and the book never says so out loud.

## Reference points

Comedians, books or writers whose register this sits near. Not to imitate - to
calibrate.

- A real consultancy scoping document or quarterly review: the form the jokes are poured into.
- The dry British sitcom register of *The Office* (UK) and *W1A*: small, true, awkward office detail played straight.
- A tired young parent's WhatsApp to her sister: the register of Lauren's feedback.
- A senior partner's one-line email reply: the register of Priya's feedback.
- Our own *Golf Addict's Guide* and *Runner's Guide*: the same deadpan programme shape, but this one is younger, set between the office and the nursery, and milder in its swearing.

## Rules - do

- Be specific. Real objects, exact numbers, exact times. "A £240 racket he has used twice, because he prefers his other £240 racket" beats "expensive equipment". Name everyday UK places and things freely; never name a real padel brand, club, player, tournament or app.
- Base jokes on real observed behaviour, not on the idea of the behaviour.
- Let the reader do the last step. Do not explain the joke.
- Dry over loud. Understatement over exclamation.
- Use the consultancy frame for structure and headings (phases, stakeholders, budgets, sign-off), and keep the sentences plain.
- Invent exact statistics about Josh ("94% of conversations lasting longer than four minutes") and report them flatly.
- Put every chapter in both worlds where it fits: something at home and something at the office.
- Slightly rude is fine where it is earned.
- Write natural prose. Full sentences. Paragraphs that breathe.

## Rules - do not

- No generic motivational language.
- No corporate register used straight ("leverage", "journey", "unlock", "synergy"). Office jargon may only appear as a quoted thing Josh says, and sparingly.
- No sentimentality, no sanitising the joke at the end.
- Do not use the "It's not X, it's Y" construction.
- Do not use three-part aphorisms as a rhythm crutch.
- Do not stack short single-sentence paragraphs for fake emphasis.
- Do not open consecutive sections with the same rhetorical move.
- No em-dash-heavy AI cadence; vary sentence construction.
- No rhetorical questions used as filler.
- No jokes about bad shots or padel technique for its own sake; padel is the setting, the people around him are the subject.
- Never make Josh a bad dad or husband, and never put the children at risk. They are only unimpressed.
- Swearing is rare and mild (bloody, sod, bollocks), saved for a punchline, and never aimed at the family.

A living list. Add to it whenever something grating survives into a draft.

- "game-changer"
- "at the end of the day"
- "let's circle back"
- "move the needle"
- "work hard, play hard"
- "boys will be boys"
- "man cave"
- "glass half full" or any other pun on the walls or the glass
- "serves him right" or any other tennis/padel pun on a normal word

## Person, tense, address

Second person, present tense, addressed to the padel addict as "you", so any padel addict can be the reader. When the programme's voice cracks it calls him Josh. Case examples and stakeholder feedback are about Josh in the third person. Stakeholder feedback is first person, past tense, headed "Stakeholder feedback (Home)" for Lauren or "Stakeholder feedback (Work)" for Priya, with a week or phase.

## Spelling and conventions

- British English throughout (-ise, colour, whilst sparingly).
- Numbers under ten spelled out except in scores, measurements, percentages and prices.
- UK words: mum, telly, tea (the evening meal), nursery, boot, buggy, queue, pint, stand-up (the Monday meeting).
- Prices in pounds (£), distances in miles.
- Times in words or the 12-hour clock ("twenty to seven", "6am"), never the 24-hour clock.
- The programme's chapters are "Phase One" to "Phase Nine", capitalised.
- Padel terms (bandeja, vibora, the glass, the wall) appear only as things Josh says or explains, never explained by the programme itself.
