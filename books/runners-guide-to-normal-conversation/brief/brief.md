## The idea in one sentence

A mock rehabilitation manual for the recreational runner who turns any ordinary question into a detailed report on their latest run.

## Target buyer

Who actually pays for this? Not a demographic - a person.

A partner, friend, relative or colleague who likes the runner but has heard one training update too many and wants a recognisable, affectionate joke gift.

## Gift recipient

Who receives it, and what is the moment it gets handed over?

The runner who brings pace, shoes, recovery scores and upcoming races into unrelated conversations. It should land as a Christmas, birthday or post-race gift.

## Recognition trigger

The "that's literally Dave" line. What specific, real behaviour will a reader
point at and say *that is exactly him*? List at least five.

1. “How was your weekend?” produces a kilometre-by-kilometre account of Sunday’s run.
2. A watch interrupts a meal, and its recovery score becomes a topic of conversation.
3. A partner knows the distinction between a recovery run and an easy run against their will.
4. Holiday accommodation is assessed for running routes before anyone discusses the holiday.
5. The runner owns many shoes and can explain the narrow purpose of each pair.
6. Colleagues have heard calf and race updates they did not request.
7. A family group chat contains more route screenshots than family photographs.

## Humour angle

What is the comedic frame? (Parody manual? Rehabilitation programme? Field
guide? Medical textbook?) Why is that frame funnier than just "jokes about X"?

The book plays the joke straight as a referral, diagnosis and rehabilitation programme. Exercises, case files and a certificate let the reader recognise their own behaviour; the calm institutional voice makes the increasingly specific habits funny. Alex is the recurring patient; Sam, Pat and Dr Hughes provide an outside view.

## Constraints

Things this book must not do. Content limits, tone limits, anything that would
embarrass the buyer when it is opened at Christmas in front of their family.

Affectionate humour rather than contempt for exercise or the recipient. This is social satire, not medical or training advice. No third-party logos or recreated app interfaces.
